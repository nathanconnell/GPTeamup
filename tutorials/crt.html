Magnetic deflection

How copper wire steers a spot of light

Inside the glass

A cathode-ray tube (CRT) has a narrow neck, a wide face and a vacuum inside. Electrons travel from the neck toward a phosphor coating on the inside of the face. Where they strike, the coating glows briefly.

The electron gun

A heater warms a cathode until it releases electrons. Electric fields accelerate them toward the screen and focus them into a narrow beam. Changing the voltage between the cathode and control grid changes how many electrons get through. More arriving per second makes a brighter spot.

The deflection yoke

Two sets of coils wrap around the glass where the neck begins to flare. Current in a winding creates a magnetic field across the beam. A moving electron feels a force sideways to both its motion and that field. Its path curves inside the yoke, then continues at the new angle toward the glass.

A vertical field bends the forward-moving beam sideways. A horizontal field bends it up or down. Reverse the coil current and the bend reverses. Increase the current and the spot lands farther from center.

Take the yoke controls

Put the spot in the upper-left corner. Then dim it without moving it. The current scales are relative, with zero at the center.

Analog video

Time becomes distance

The horizontal coil current ramps from one polarity to the other, sweeping the spot from left to right. Then it swings back quickly for the next line. On a graph, that repeated ramp and return looks like a sawtooth. The beam is blanked during the return so it leaves no bright diagonal streak.

The vertical coils run a much slower sweep from top to bottom. The resulting pattern of lines is called a raster. In U.S. analog color television, the set traced about 15,734 lines per second. One field traced every other line. The next filled the gaps, producing about 30 interlaced frames per second.

What came through the yellow video jack?

Consider a black-and-white picture sent as composite video. During the visible part of a line, the changing signal voltage represents a continuous range from black through gray to white. Sync pulses in the blanking intervals give the receiver timing references. The TV’s own circuits supply the much larger currents that drive the coils.

Read one scan line

Imagine the beam crossing a softly lit moon. The gold trace shows the brightness signal over time. The strip below shows the line it paints. Scrub through the visible part of the line to connect a moment with a place.

Why would a picture roll vertically?

If the vertical sweep runs slightly faster or slower than the incoming fields, each new picture starts at a different height. A stationary face appears to climb or sink. Locking the vertical sweep to the incoming sync pulses holds it still.

Color tubes

Why a magnet could turn a face green

A color TV decodes brightness and color information into three drive signals. Three electron beams approach the screen from slightly different angles. In a shadow-mask tube, a thin perforated metal sheet lets each beam reach its own red, green or blue phosphor. The phosphor supplies the color. The yoke sweeps all three beams together.

A stray magnetic field can bend a beam just enough to hit the wrong phosphor. A magnetized mask can keep the colored patch there even after the external field is gone. Many sets had a separate degaussing coil around the tube face. At cold startup its alternating field gradually weakened, reducing residual magnetization.
